# TJ's Writing Style Guide

> Reference for agent to match tone and voice.

## Voice Characteristics

- **Genuine** — Authentic, not performative. Mean what you say.
- **Warm** — Supportive and human. The person reading this should feel valued.
- **Direct** — Get to the point. No hedging or burying feedback.
- **Inspiring** — Connect work to growth and potential.

## What to Avoid

- Flowery language or excessive adjectives
- Corporate buzzwords and empty phrases
- Hedging or softening direct feedback unnecessarily
- Generic praise that could apply to anyone

## Extracted Patterns

### Accomplishments

**Structure**: Project context → Person's role → Why it mattered → Specific behaviors

Example patterns:
> "During [Project], [Name] quickly [action] and eventually [ownership]. His contributions were critically important in [business impact]."

> "As a core contributor to [Project], [Name] successfully delivered [outcome]. Although faced with [challenge], [Name] teamed up with [collaborator] to [result]."

> "As the sole technical lead for [component], [Name] developed and launched [feature], effectively [impact]."

**Characteristics**:
- Lead with project/context, not the person
- Use specific verbs: "onboarded", "co-owned", "leveraged", "executed", "developed", "launched"
- Acknowledge challenges overcome: "Although faced with a tight turnaround..."
- Connect to business outcomes: "unblocking UK, Brazil expansion", "one of the most impactful changes to date"
- Acknowledge quality: "meticulously worked through", "thorough approach", "incredibly thorough"
- End with concrete delivery: "on time for the Partner Beta"
- Name collaborators when teamwork was key
- Use parentheses for inline clarifications, not em-dashes: "identified a critical gap (repeat dodgers were not being re-reviewed)" rather than "identified a critical gap—repeat dodgers..."
- Connect behaviors to established character patterns: "Typical of his approach, Charles left no stone unturned..." — this reinforces that the behavior is consistent, not a one-off
- End accomplishment paragraphs with *how* the person worked or led, not with a business outcome statement: "his flexible approach to the project team structure and communication kept the group moving with confidence" rather than "the result strengthens partner trust"
- Add brief context on *why* a project existed, not just what was delivered: "a retention play caused by the change in partnership between Alea and an affiliated prohost group"
- Explain *what* made something hard, not just *who* was involved: "navigating all the external approvals necessary for sharing guest PII" rather than "navigating Legal, Privacy, and Security approvals"
- Don't over-specify when concise works — if a point is already made, don't pad it with extra names or details

### Behaviors

**Structure**: Background/experience → How it manifests → Growth/recognition

Example patterns:
> "Drawing on his time as [background], [Name] has an extraordinarily well rounded perspective on [domain]. When he takes on [work], it's this experience and empathy that [specific behavior]."

> "[Name] also developed and presented options to address [problem]. When faced with [challenge] at launch time, [Name] was communicative and decisive in mitigating [issue], while researching a permanent fix."

**Characteristics**:
- Connect the whole person (past roles, unique experiences)
- Explain the "why" behind their approach
- Acknowledge team perception: "trust has increased rapidly"
- Use genuine superlatives sparingly: "incredible to see", "extraordinarily", "incredibly thorough"
- Highlight specific behavioral traits: "communicative and decisive", "thorough"
- Credit proactive quality work: testing, escalating defects, adding coverage
- Don't repeat background details already established in prior years' reviews — use "continues to" to acknowledge continuity: "Charles's ops background continues to give him" rather than re-explaining the ops/resident host backstory
- Don't over-explain or add summary sentences — trust the reader to draw conclusions from the examples provided
- Avoid repeating points across sections; if something was highlighted as an accomplishment, don't restate it in behaviors
- Lead with behavioral characterization, not project structure: "ability to lead by example helped lift all of our squad's projects" over "milestone-driven approach to project planning defined his year"
- Use peer feedback as direct evidence for behavioral claims: "It's a big reason Drew's teammates described the alignment as 'seamless'" — attribute the characterization to peers, not just you
- Use analogy/metaphor sparingly for memorable emphasis: "If design, product, and engineering are the three legs of the stool, Drew showed his ability to keep the stool level and sturdy"

### Growth Areas

**Structure**: Specific opportunity → Rationale (why this matters) → Positive reframe

Example patterns:
> "I'd especially like to see [Name] seek out opportunities that allow him to [growth area]."

> "Because [Name] cares deeply about [thing], he can tend to [behavior]. [Reframe as strength/path forward]."

> "Reflecting on some challenging moments, [Name] should focus on [area]. [Specific actions] will build others' confidence in her work. She is already making significant strides in this area."

> "I'd also be thrilled to see [Name] exercise more ownership of [domain]. This involves seeking opportunities to [specific actions]."

**Characteristics**:
- First person: "I'd like to see", "I want [Name] to", "I'd be thrilled to see"
- Frame as opportunities, not deficits
- Use quotes for emphasis: "Deciding what not to do is as important as deciding what to do."
- Explain the stakes: "risk burning himself out", "build others' confidence"
- End on growth potential, not criticism
- Acknowledge progress already made: "She is already making significant strides"
- Be specific about what growth looks like: "tech lead opportunities on small to medium-sized projects"
- Connect growth to showcasing existing strengths
- Use "we" when referencing past feedback — it signals shared responsibility and makes feedback feel collaborative: "The pattern we flagged last year" rather than "The pattern I flagged"
- Be specific about the trajectory of recurring patterns: "appeared to worsen at times this year" is more honest and useful than "came to bear"
- Give specific, actionable guidance — not just what to change, but how: "Keeping problem statements and solutions simple and approachable, as well as demonstrating iterative progress towards the complete solution" rather than just "keep solutions simple"
- Keep statements personal to this employee; avoid generic truisms: "a healthy mix of work where he can still feel expert" rather than "Engineers learning new stacks need..."
- Use direct, honest characterizations — "needed a push in order to follow through" reads truer than "didn't always choose to"; "seems dismissive or impatient" is more accurate than "sometimes interrupts"
- Reframe peer desire to collaborate as evidence of respect: "Drew's teammates cited a desire to be involved earlier — another sign of their eagerness to collaborate with him" turns a gap into a vote of confidence
- Use reputational framing for interpersonal growth feedback — "I don't want [Name] to earn a reputation as..." makes the stakes concrete without feeling like an attack
- Acknowledge specific trade-offs when asking someone to take a risk: "even when that delegated work might fail" — naming the vulnerability makes the ask feel real, not theoretical
- Use "superpower" or similar vivid language sparingly to cast a growth area as potential: "will become his superpower if exercised on a more consistent basis"
- Close growth sections on optimism — cut additional criticism that would leave the section ending on a low note; end with a forward-looking sentence
- Close a growth note with peer validation when available: "This is the behavior [Name]'s peers most want to see" — grounds the ask in team signal, not just manager judgment

## Sentence Patterns

- Medium-length sentences, often compound with "and"
- Mix of declarative statements and observations
- Active voice with strong verbs
- Occasional rhetorical emphasis through quotes

## Vocabulary TJ Uses

**Praise**: critically important, deeply impressed, thorough, meticulously, extraordinarily, incredible, foundational, keystone, crucial, incredibly thorough, one of the most impactful, communicative and decisive, significant strides

**Actions**: onboarded, co-owned, leveraged, executed, navigated, delivered, positioned, drove, led, developed, launched, teamed up, escalated, mitigated, presented options

**Growth framing**: I'd especially like to see, I want [Name] to, I'd be thrilled to see, seek out opportunities, carve out time, be more selective, more comfortable, should focus on, exercise more ownership, showcase and grow, I agree that, I'm looking forward to working with him/her in these areas, without fear of stepping on other's toes, will become his/her superpower, I don't want [Name] to earn a reputation as, even when that delegated work might fail, another sign of their eagerness to collaborate

**Gratitude/acknowledgment**: I'm incredibly grateful for, the team would have benefited from, teammates appreciate, gave everyone confidence

**Trait references**: characteristic tenacity, exceptional relationship-building skills, displayed his/her characteristic [trait]

**Critical feedback**: we've seen [Name] inadvertently [issue], the team would have benefited from more [behavior], when he/she pushes too far ahead without bringing others along, needed a push in order to follow through, seems dismissive or impatient, this is the behavior [Name]'s peers most want to see
